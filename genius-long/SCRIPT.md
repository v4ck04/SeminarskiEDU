## Facts and sources
**Isaac Newton, 1720.** Master of the Royal Mint (in the Tower of London) since 1699, aged 77. South Sea shares: about £128 in
January, £330 in March, £550 at the end of May, £890 in early June, £1,000 in early August, £150 at the end of September.
Newton sold in April for a profit of about £20,000, bought back in June near the peak with most of his fortune, and ended with
about £20,000 from just over £30,000 before the bubble. He wrote: "I lost very much by the South Sea company which makes my
pockets empty". The Chancellor of the Exchequer, John Aislabie, was sent to the Tower in March 1721.
Sources: A. Odlyzko, "Isaac Newton and the perils of the financial South Sea", Physics Today, July 2020; Wikipedia, "South Sea Company", "John Aislabie".
The April price on the chart (about £350) is an approximation between the March and May figures.

**Mark Twain, 1880–1894.** Put about $300,000 (roughly $8–10 million today; the figure is the usual estimate, sources range
from $150,000 to $300,000) into James W. Paige's typesetting machine between 1880 and 1894; the machine had about 18,000
parts and was overtaken by the Linotype (in newspaper use from 1886). His publishing house (Charles L. Webster & Co., publisher
of Grant's memoirs) failed and he filed for bankruptcy in April 1894; from July 1895 he made a lecture tour around the world
and repaid his creditors in full although he was not legally required to. The only surviving machine is at the Mark Twain
House in Hartford. The telephone story is Twain's own (his autobiography), hence "by his own account".
Sources: Wikipedia, "Mark Twain", "Paige Compositor"; Mark Twain Project (biography of James Paige); Linda Hall Library.

**Irving Fisher, 1929.** Yale professor; sold his index-card filing system to Kardex Rand in 1925; fortune reported at about
$10 million; heavily margined. On 15 October 1929 he said stock prices had reached "what looks like a permanently high
plateau"; the crash began on 24 October; on 28 and 29 October the Dow fell 12.8% and 11.7% (about 23% in two days); from 381 on
3 September 1929 it fell to 41 on 8 July 1932 (−89%). He lost the fortune, borrowed from his sister-in-law, and Yale bought his
house and rented it back to him. His debt-deflation theory is still in use.
Sources: Wikipedia, "Irving Fisher"; L. H. White, "The Clash of Economic Ideas" (Cambridge University Press); the Fisher bibliography at cs.nyu.edu/davise.
The Dow levels and the two daily falls are the standard published figures; they were not re-checked online in this session.

**Long-Term Capital Management, 1994–1998.** Founded in 1994 by John Meriwether; Myron Scholes and Robert Merton (Nobel Prize
1997) on the board. Returns after fees of about 21%, 43% and 41% in its first three years. At the start of 1998: equity $4.7
billion, borrowings over $124.5 billion (more than 25 to 1). Russia defaulted on 17 August 1998; the fund lost $4.6 billion in
less than four months; on 23 September 1998 fourteen institutions put in about $3.6 billion under the supervision of the
Federal Reserve Bank of New York; the partners' own $1.9 billion was wiped out; the fund was dissolved in early 2000.
Meriwether's later fund (JWM Partners) closed in 2009 after losses in the 2008 crisis (from memory, not re-checked online).
Sources: Wikipedia, "Long-Term Capital Management"; CFA Institute blog (2013); R. Lowenstein, "When Genius Failed".

**Ronald Read.** See read-short/SCRIPT.md (same facts: 25 years at a service station, 17 as a janitor, at least 95 stocks,
almost $8 million, $4.8 million to the hospital and $1.2 million to the library, the largest gifts either had received;
certificates stacked five inches high; shares held since 1959).

Left out on purpose: the "madness of people" quote attributed to Newton (no contemporary source), and the Nvidia story (still developing).

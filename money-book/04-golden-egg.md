# Chapter 4
# One Golden Egg Explains It

### *Inflation, and why "safe" savings shrink*

> If you hid a hundred dollars under your bed in the year 2000, today it buys only about half as much.
> Nobody stole it. So where did the other half go?

---

## The Story

In the river town, money is golden eggs.

Every morning the three men fish. Every evening they carry their baskets to the market, and every basket of fish costs
exactly one golden egg. It has cost one golden egg for as long as anyone can remember. Nobody thinks about it, the same
way nobody thinks about the river.

Then, one spring, the mayor finds a goose.

It is a plain white goose, a little bad-tempered, and it lays golden eggs. Not one a year. Several a day.

The mayor is a generous man, and he likes being liked. So he does what any generous man would do with a goose like
that. He walks down to the landing stage with his arms full of eggs, and he hands them out. One for the baker. Two for
the woman who mends nets. Three for the children, because why not?

For a week, the whole town feels rich. People walk around with eggs in both hands. They laugh more. They say the mayor
is the best mayor the town has ever had.

But the river doesn't care about the goose.

The river still gives the same fish. The three men still catch the same three baskets a day. There are more eggs in
town than ever, and exactly the same amount of fish.

So something has to give.

On Monday, a woman with a pocket full of eggs wants a basket of fish so badly that she offers two eggs instead of one.
The fisherman takes it. Why wouldn't he? By Wednesday, everyone has heard. A basket now costs two eggs. By the end of
the month, it costs three.

Nobody decided this. There was no meeting. The fishermen didn't get greedy. There were simply more eggs chasing the
same fish, and each egg was worth a little less.

Now look at the three men.

**The second man** had been careful for years. Every week he put one egg into a glass jar on his shelf. He had a
hundred eggs in that jar. A hundred eggs used to mean a hundred baskets of fish: a whole season of food, a cushion for a
bad winter. He had never spent a single one.

He still has a hundred eggs. Nobody touched the jar. But a hundred eggs now buy about thirty-three baskets.

He did everything right, and two-thirds of his savings disappeared without anyone taking a thing.

**The first man** had borrowed fifty eggs from the lender a year earlier, to buy a new net. He'd lost sleep over that
debt. Fifty eggs used to be fifty baskets of fish: months of work.

But the debt is still written as fifty eggs. And eggs are cheap now. He sells a few baskets of fish at the new price,
walks over to the lender, and pays back every egg he owes.

The lender counts the eggs, and frowns. They're all there. They're just not worth what they used to be.

**The third man** never kept many eggs at all. Whenever he had extra, he turned them into things: a second boat, a
better net, a share of the smokehouse. Things that catch fish, carry fish, or keep fish.

When the price of fish went from one egg to three, the price of his fish went from one egg to three. When people wanted
to buy his second boat, they offered three times what he'd paid. His things didn't lose value. They just got a new
price tag, in cheaper eggs.

At the end of the year, the town is full of eggs. They are in the jar, in the market, in the corners of the landing
stage where children dropped them. The goose is still laying.

The eggs didn't get stolen. There were just more of them.

## What Really Happens

The river town is a cartoon, but the mechanism is real. In the real world it has a name: **inflation**, a general rise
in prices over time. Another way to say it is that each dollar buys a little less than it used to.

### The real numbers

The United States measures prices with the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**: the cost of a big basket of everyday things,
like food, rent, fuel, clothes and medical care, tracked every month by the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

- In **2000**, the index averaged **172.2**.
- In **September 2026**, it stood at **336.9**.

That is almost exactly **double**. Something that cost $100 in 2000 costs about $196 today. Turned around: a $100 bill
saved in 2000 now buys what about $51 bought back then.

In the twelve months to September 2026, prices rose **3.7%**. That sounds small. But at 3.7% a year, prices double in
about nineteen years.

> **A quick shortcut: the Rule of 72.** Divide 72 by a yearly percentage, and you get roughly how many years it takes
> for something to double. At 3.7% inflation, 72 ÷ 3.7 ≈ 19 years. At 2%, it's 36 years. At 7%, it's about 10.

### Why prices rise

The goose shows one cause: **more money, same amount of stuff.** When there is more money chasing the same goods,
sellers can ask for more, and buyers will pay it.

But it's not the only cause, and the real world is messier than the river town:

- **Costs go up.** If fuel, wages or materials get more expensive, businesses raise prices to cover them.
- **Supply drops.** A bad harvest, a war, a blocked shipping route: fewer goods, same demand, higher prices.
- **Everyone expects it.** If people believe prices will rise next year, workers ask for raises now and businesses
  raise prices now, which helps make it happen.

Most of the time, several of these are happening at once.

### Is inflation always bad?

No. A little inflation is normal, and most central banks aim for it on purpose. The U.S. Federal Reserve, for example,
aims for inflation of about **2% a year** over the long run. Steady, low inflation encourages people to spend and invest
instead of hoarding cash, and it gives the economy some room to adjust.

The danger is when inflation is high, or rises fast, or when you don't notice what it's doing to the money you've set
aside.

## Who Wins, Who Loses

Inflation doesn't hit everyone the same way. In the story, three men lived through the same rise in prices and ended up
in three very different places. Here is why.

### People holding cash: usually lose

**The second man's jar.** Cash under a mattress, in a drawer, or in an account that pays little or no interest loses
buying power every year that prices rise. The number on the paper stays the same. What it can buy shrinks.

This is the cruel part of inflation: it punishes the careful. The second man didn't gamble or overspend. He just held
something that slowly lost value.

> **Your real return.** If your savings earn 1% a year and prices rise 3.7%, your money buys about 2.7% less every
> year. That's your *real* return: interest minus inflation. A positive number on your bank statement can still be a
> loss.

### People with fixed-rate debt: can win

**The first man's fifty eggs.** If you owe a fixed amount, inflation makes that amount easier to pay back over time,
because your income, in a world of rising prices, tends to rise too. A fixed-rate mortgage from twenty years ago looks
much smaller next to today's paychecks.

But be careful with this one:

- It works for **fixed-rate** debt. Many loans, including most credit cards, have **variable** rates that rise when
  inflation rises. Then the debt gets more expensive, not less.
- It only helps if your income actually keeps up. If prices rise and your pay doesn't, debt gets harder to carry, not
  easier.

Inflation is not a reason to borrow. It just changes the math of debt you already have.

### People who own things that produce value: tend to keep up

**The third man's boats.** Assets that produce something people need (a business, a share of a company, a property,
a skill people pay for) tend to rise in price along with everything else over long periods, because what they produce
rises in price too.

"Tend to" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Owning things comes with its own risks: prices of assets swing up
and down, sometimes hard, and a boat can sink. Chapter 3 is about what happens when you buy those things with borrowed
money. Chapter 12 covers the basics of owning them sensibly.

But the lesson of the third man is simple: **money that sits still slowly shrinks. Money turned into something useful
has a chance to keep up.**

## Your Move

**One thing to do this week: find your real return.**

1. Look up the interest rate on the account where your savings sit. It's in your banking app or on your statement,
   usually called *APY* or *interest rate*.
2. Look up your country's latest yearly inflation rate. Search "*[your country]* inflation rate" and use your national
   statistics office.
3. Subtract: **your interest rate − inflation = your real return.**

If the answer is negative, your savings are quietly shrinking, like the second man's jar.

That does **not** mean you should empty your savings into something risky. Money you might need soon, like an emergency
fund or rent, *should* be safe and easy to reach, even if it shrinks a little (Chapter 11 is about exactly this). It
just means you now know what "safe" really costs you, and you can decide on purpose how much belongs in the jar and how
much could one day become a boat.

## Remember

- Inflation is a general rise in prices: each dollar buys a little less over time.
- U.S. prices almost doubled from 2000 to 2026. $100 saved in 2000 buys about what $51 did then.
- Cash that earns less than inflation slowly loses buying power.
- Fixed-rate debt gets easier to repay over time. Variable-rate debt can get harder.
- Things that produce value tend to keep up with prices over long periods, but they carry their own risks.
- The eggs didn't get stolen. There were just more of them.
